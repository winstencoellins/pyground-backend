# Loops and Nested Data

Dictionaries really shine when you combine them with loops. In this lesson you'll walk
through a dictionary, count things, and build a small report.

## `keys()`, `values()`, and `items()`

- `keys()` gives all the keys. A plain `for k in d` loop also gives the keys.
- `values()` gives all the values.
- `items()` gives the key and value pairs together.

<Playground initialCode={`scores = {"Budi": 85, "Ani": 92, "Citra": 78}

for name in scores.keys():
    print("Student:", name)

print("Total score:", sum(scores.values()))

for name, score in scores.items():
    print(f"{name} got {score}")`} />

<Callout type="tip">
  `items()` is the most common choice. You get two variables at once, just like
  `enumerate()` on a list.
</Callout>

## Counting with a dictionary

A very common pattern: a dictionary as a **counter**. The key is the thing being counted and
the value is how many. `get(key, 0)` keeps the first addition safe:

<Playground initialCode={`orders = ["es teh", "kopi", "es teh", "jus", "es teh", "kopi"]
counts = {}

for drink in orders:
    counts[drink] = counts.get(drink, 0) + 1

print(counts)

sentence = "i like coffee and i like tea"
word_counts = {}
for word in sentence.split():
    word_counts[word] = word_counts.get(word, 0) + 1
print(word_counts)`} />

## A list of dictionaries

Real-world data often looks like a **list of dictionaries**: each dictionary is one record.
For example, a list of ojek rides:

<Playground initialCode={`rides = [
    {"driver": "Dodi", "distance": 5, "fare": 15000},
    {"driver": "Budi", "distance": 3, "fare": 10000},
    {"driver": "Dodi", "distance": 8, "fare": 22000},
]

total = 0
for r in rides:
    print(r["driver"], "rode", r["distance"], "km")
    total = total + r["fare"]

print("Total fares: Rp", total)
print("Driver of the second ride:", rides[1]["driver"])`} />

Look at `rides[1]["driver"]`: the index `[1]` picks the second dictionary, then
`["driver"]` picks its value.

## A dictionary of lists

The other way round works too: dictionary values can be lists. That's handy for grouping
data, such as a class cleaning rota by day:

<Playground initialCode={`rota = {
    "Monday": ["Ani", "Budi"],
    "Tuesday": ["Citra"],
}

rota["Tuesday"].append("Dodi")
rota["Wednesday"] = ["Eka", "Fajar"]

for day, people in rota.items():
    print(day, ":", ", ".join(people))`} />

`", ".join(people)` combines the list items into one string, with `", "` between each item.

## Printing a tidy report

Put it all together with f-strings. The format `:<10` left-aligns text in 10 characters and
`:>8` right-aligns in 8, so the columns line up:

<Playground initialCode={`sales = {"nasi goreng": 12, "mie ayam": 8, "soto": 15}
prices = {"nasi goreng": 15000, "mie ayam": 12000, "soto": 13000}

print("WARUNG REPORT FOR TODAY")
print("-" * 32)
total = 0
for dish, portions in sales.items():
    revenue = portions * prices[dish]
    total = total + revenue
    print(f"{dish:<12}{portions:>4} plates  Rp{revenue:>8}")
print("-" * 32)
print(f"{'Total':<25}Rp{total:>8}")`} />

## Summary

- `for k in d` and `d.keys()` give keys, `d.values()` gives values, `d.items()` gives both.
- Counter pattern: `d[x] = d.get(x, 0) + 1`.
- A list of dictionaries suits a list of records; a dictionary of lists suits grouping.
- The f-string formats `:<10` and `:>8` give you a report with neat columns.
